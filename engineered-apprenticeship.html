The Problem
Why the Course Was Never the Engine

For decades, the 70-20-10 model described where competence actually came from: 70% from on-the-job experience, 20% from others, 10% from formal courses. L&D owned the 10%. The other 90% ran on autopilot — routine tasks accumulated the reps, proximity to senior colleagues transmitted the judgment, and feedback loops closed naturally through the flow of live work.

Nobody designed this. It happened as a side effect of how knowledge work was structured. New associates sat near partners. Junior tax professionals did the groundwork that senior ones reviewed. The informal apprenticeship was free, invisible, and doing most of the development work.

The critical insight is that transmitting knowledge was never the binding constraint on competence. Accumulating enough first-hand pattern experience to develop judgment was. Courses were never the engine — they were the visible part of a much larger system that was doing the real work in the background.

AI has dismantled this system in two ways. First, it has absorbed the routine tasks that created most of the pattern experience — the document reviews, the first-draft decks, the repetitive analysis. Junior staff no longer accumulate the reps, because AI is doing the reps. Second, it has hidden the senior reasoning that used to be visible. When a partner uses AI to produce an output, the chain of thinking that would previously have been observable — through proximity, through reviewed drafts, through corridor conversation — is now inside the model's black box.

Both the reps and the reasoning have been switched off simultaneously. The informal apprenticeship, which was invisible precisely because it required no design, has collapsed. What L&D now faces is not a marginal expansion of scope. It is responsibility for the entire capability development system — the 100%, not the 10%.

Case Studies
What the Engineered Apprenticeship Looks Like in Practice

Four builds from 2025-2026. Different sectors, same design logic.

🏛️
Big Law — DepoSim
Six firms, 160+ hours, 97% approval — practising what AI can't do
▾

Six US firms (Orrick, K&L Gates, McDermott, Littler, Taft, Brownstein) participated. Built by AltaClaro + Verbit. AI agents play the witness, opposing counsel, and court reporter simultaneously. Associates must read the situation and make real-time decisions under realistic pressure.

160+ hours have been piloted. 97% of participants strongly agreed the simulation was valuable. One litigation chair ran it live in front of 500 attorneys as a demonstration of what practice-centred training looks like at scale.

This isn't a simulation of document review — AI's domain. It's simulation of the deposition: the human judgment call that requires reading a room, adapting live, and making calls that AI cannot reliably make for you.
"Mentor–mentee pairs use its feedback to start coaching conversations." — the simulator becomes the prompt for human-to-human learning.
Litigation Simulation 6 firms
⚖️
Arbitration — Atelier
Skill that used to take years now practised on day one
▾

Built by Three Crowns arbitration firm with Dr Megan Ma at Stanford Law's LIFT Lab. Junior associates rehearse cross-examining witnesses, adapting in real time as the AI witness responds in unexpected ways — a skill that previously required years of courtroom experience to practise under pressure.

The build represents an emerging design principle: simulate the performance-critical professional decisions, but keep human-to-human learning — the culture, unwritten rules, client presence — in the room. Simulators can replicate decision pressure; they cannot replicate a profession's social fabric.

Skills built on recognition — reading a witness, sensing when an argument is landing — can be practised in a simulator and learned faster there than through years of live exposure. The simulator compresses the experience curve.
"Skills built on recognition can be practised in a simulator, and learned faster there than through years of live work."
Arbitration Stanford Recognition skills

KPMG — TaxSIM (10,000 people)
A national bet that designed practice can replace the reps clients used to fund
▾

TaxSIM built with Centaurian AI. Rolling out to approximately 10,000 US tax professionals across KPMG's national practice. The programme is designed to give junior professionals the skills that used to take years of repetitive client work — work that AI now absorbs before juniors can practise it.

The routine reps that AI has absorbed were not just tasks — they were the practice that used to develop competence. TaxSIM is KPMG's answer to a structural gap: if clients no longer fund junior development through repetitive work, who does?

KPMG is training juniors to work as "managers of agents" — a title that implies supervising AI output rather than doing the underlying computation. The entire training model has had to shift to match a redefined entry-level job.
"Not a pilot in a training suite — it's a bet, across a national practice." The scale signals strategic conviction, not experimentation.
Tax Scale KPMG
🔍
Deloitte & PwC — Graduate Redesign
First-years doing what used to take five years — reviewing AI from week one
▾

Deloitte is rebuilding its 3-year audit graduate scheme around simulated scenarios from September — replacing a programme structured around live client exposure with one structured around deliberate practice. PwC's first-year hires are expected to work as AI reviewers immediately upon joining.

Within 3 years, according to PwC, first-year associates will feel like the managers of a previous generation — because client-facing responsibility, which once took years to earn, is being handed to them from week one.

The entry-level job has been redefined. Mid-career skills — scepticism, knowing when plausible is wrong, professional judgment, knowing when to escalate — must now be taught from day one because client-facing responsibility no longer waits three years.
"The training is changing to match a redefined entry-level job." The locus of change is not the learning programme — it's the nature of entry-level professional work.

The Anatomy
Three Design Moves That Appear in Every Build

Across DepoSim, Atelier, TaxSIM, and the Big Four redesigns — the same three structural choices appear each time.

🔁
Design Move 1 — The Loop
Attempt → Compare → Retry — replacing years of case accumulation
▾

Every build puts the learner in a realistic situation and forces a decision before showing any answer. Then shows what an expert would have done and why. Then goes again — harder, with less scaffolding. The structure is identical across law, tax, and audit. The content is domain-specific; the loop is universal.

The loop's specific purpose: building enough first-hand pattern experience to judge AI's work without needing years of live cases. You cannot teach someone to spot a wrong answer by describing wrong answers. They must have experienced being wrong — and understood why — enough times to have calibrated intuition.
"A course starts from 'what do they need to know?' These builds start from 'which calls make someone good at this job?'"
Deliberate practice Pattern recognition
🧠
Design Move 2 — Expert Reasoning, Captured on Purpose
The most valuable new skill in L&D: getting reasoning OUT of experts' heads
▾

The old apprenticeship passed on judgment by proximity — you sat near the partner for years. The reasoning was transmitted through observation, correction, and gradual exposure. AI ended this: when the partner's reasoning goes into the model's output, the chain of thinking is no longer observable.

Every build now requires the opposite: experts explaining their thinking on purpose, so it can be captured and turned into simulator logic, feedback rubrics, and example decisions. This is the work that learning designers now need to do — and it is craft, not administration.

This is a new competence that most L&D professionals have never practised. Training taught us to collect content from subject matter experts. The new skill is getting experts to narrate their decision-making — to make tacit judgment explicit enough to be modelled, tested against, and improved.
"Getting reasoning out of experts' heads used to happen by accident, through seating plans. Now it has to happen on purpose."
SME elicitation Expertise
🤖
Design Move 3 — Judging AI's Work from Day One
At PwC, reviewing AI output IS the entry-level job now
▾

On routine tasks, AI narrows the competence gap between novices and experts (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025 call centre study). On complex tasks that require domain judgment, the effect is the opposite: AI widens the gap. Experts use AI to go further, faster. Novices accept plausible-but-generic output because they lack the pattern recognition to distinguish it from genuinely good work (An, 2026).

At PwC, reviewing AI output is not a future task or an advanced skill — it is the entry-level job. New hires are given AI reviewer responsibility from week one. The implication for training is direct: mid-career skills (scepticism, calibrated escalation, professional judgment about when plausible is wrong) must be developed from day one.

Giving juniors AI tools without teaching them to judge AI's work doesn't close the competence gap. It compounds it. This is the risk hiding inside the most generous AI access policies.
"On complex tasks that need domain judgment, AI widens the gap: experts use it to go further, novices accept plausible-but-generic output."
